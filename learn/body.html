🧠 Understand it

1Eczema (Sean): what it is and the daily routine6 key points
  • Eczema (atopic dermatitis) is two problems at once: a leaky skin barrier and an immune system that overreacts.
  • A weak barrier lets water out and irritants in. Many people carry gene changes (like filaggrin, a skin-building protein) that make the barrier thinner.
  • Itch leads to scratching, which damages skin more, which itches more. Breaking that loop is the whole game.
  • Moisturisers (emollients) are the base treatment, not an extra. The NHS says use them at least twice a day, generously, even when skin looks clear.
  • Soak and seal: short lukewarm bath or shower, pat dry, then put moisturiser on within about 3 minutes so it locks the water in.
  • Common triggers: soap and bubble bath, sweat, heat, wool, stress, dust mites, and fragranced products. Swap soap for an emollient wash.
2Eczema (Sean): medicines that work, and diet myths6 key points
  • Steroid creams (topical corticosteroids) calm flares fast. Used in the right strength, for short bursts, they are safe. Fear of them often causes under-treatment.
  • Dose by fingertip unit (a line of cream from fingertip to first crease). One unit covers about two adult palms of skin.
  • Calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus, pimecrolimus) are steroid-free creams. They are good for the face, eyelids and folds where steroids thin the skin.
  • For severe eczema, a dermatologist can offer injections (biologics like dupilumab) or tablets (JAK inhibitors). These work well but are costly in SA, so check your medical aid.
  • Diet: the NHS says do not cut out foods unless a doctor tells you to. Food allergy matters more in babies; in adults, elimination diets rarely help and can backfire.
  • Real evidence: a Cochrane review found moisturisers mean fewer flares and less steroid needed. Most 'miracle' creams were no better than a plain one.
3High blood pressure (Sean's mom): the numbers and why it's silent6 key points
  • Blood pressure has two numbers. The top (systolic) is pressure when the heart squeezes. The bottom (diastolic) is pressure when it rests.
  • Healthy is roughly 90/60 to 120/80. High is 140/90 or more at a clinic, or 135/85 or more at home (NHS).
  • It is called silent because most people feel nothing. WHO says about 44% of adults with high blood pressure do not know they have it.
  • Over years, high pressure damages arteries, which leads to stroke, heart attack, kidney failure and dementia. Treatment cuts those risks.
  • South Africans eat about 6 to 11 g of salt a day. WHO's limit is under 5 g, about one teaspoon. Much of it hides in bread, stock cubes, processed meat and takeaways.
  • SA law has capped salt in processed foods since 2016, which helped, but home cooking and stock cubes still add a lot.
4High blood pressure: what actually lowers it6 key points
  • DASH (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension) is an eating plan with lots of vegetables, fruit, beans, whole grains and low-fat dairy, and less salt and fatty meat. Trials show it lowers pressure.
  • Exercise helps: aim for about 150 minutes a week of brisk walking or similar.
  • Alcohol raises blood pressure. Cutting down helps; heavy drinking is a big driver.
  • Home checks need a validated upper-arm machine (one tested for accuracy; check the STRIDE BP list). Sit 5 minutes, arm at heart level, take 2 readings morning and evening for a week.
  • Pills matter. Most people need them for life. Stopping because you feel fine can make pressure bounce back up, because feeling fine was never the sign.
  • Emergency signs: very high readings (around 180/120) with chest pain, severe headache, confusion, vision loss or weakness. Face drooping or slurred speech means stroke. Call 112 from a mobile or 10177.
5Acne (sister-in-law): causes, what works, myths and scars6 key points
  • Acne starts when oil (sebum) and dead skin clog a pore, bacteria (C. acnes) grow, and the spot gets inflamed. Hormones drive the oil. It is not caused by dirty skin.
  • First line: benzoyl peroxide (kills bacteria, no resistance) and retinoids like adapalene (unclog pores). Start thin, every other night, and give it 8 to 12 weeks. Azelaic acid is gentler and fades dark marks.
  • Antibiotics should never be used alone or long-term. NICE says pair them with benzoyl peroxide or a retinoid and review by about 3 months to limit resistance.
  • For women, the combined pill or spironolactone can help. Severe or scarring acne needs a dermatologist for isotretinoin, which works very well but needs strict pregnancy prevention and monitoring.
  • Diet: modest evidence that high-GI foods (sugary, white-flour foods that spike blood sugar) and milk, especially skim, worsen acne in some people. Chocolate alone has weak evidence. It's a nudge, not a cure.
  • Picking causes scars; early treatment prevents them. Dark marks (post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation) fade over months with sunscreen. Pitted scars need dermatologist procedures.
6Drawing blood: how much, how fast it comes back, how to read it6 key points
  • An adult has about 5 litres of blood (about 8% of body weight). A test tube holds about 2 to 10 ml, so a normal panel of a few tubes is well under 1% of your blood.
  • A full donation is much bigger: SANBS takes 480 ml, about 10%. Even that is safe for healthy adults.
  • Plasma (the watery part of blood) comes back within a day or two if you drink fluids. Red cells take weeks; haemoglobin is usually back to normal in 6 to 12 weeks.
  • Draws matter when blood is already low: anaemia (too few red cells or low haemoglobin), babies, and long hospital stays where daily tests can add up.
  • FBC (full blood count) basics: haemoglobin (oxygen carrier), MCV (red cell size: small often means low iron, big can mean low B12 or folate), white cells (infection and immunity), platelets (clotting).
  • Reference ranges cover about 95% of healthy people, so a slightly flagged result is not automatically a problem. Trends over time and how you feel matter more.

✅ What we actually do

❓ Questions to ask

Screenshot these or read them out.

  1. Which strength of steroid cream should I use, on which body parts, and for how many days?
  2. Is my eczema bad enough for a dermatologist, or for biologic or JAK treatment, and does my medical aid cover it?
  3. What is my mom's target blood pressure, and how should she time her pills?
  4. Could any of her other medicines (like anti-inflammatory painkillers) be pushing her blood pressure up?
  5. Is this acne bad enough for isotretinoin, and what monitoring would that need?
  6. Which of my blood results are out of range, by how much, and do they need retesting?

⚠️ Watch out for

  • Myth: steroid creams always thin your skin. Short courses at the right strength are safe; under-treating eczema causes more harm.
  • Red flag: eczema that suddenly weeps, crusts or forms painful clustered blisters, with fever. That can be infection or eczema herpeticum and needs a doctor the same day.
  • Myth: 'I feel fine, so I can stop my blood pressure pills.' High pressure has no feeling. Never stop without the doctor.
  • Red flag: severe headache, chest pain, sudden vision change, face drooping or slurred speech. Call 112 or 10177 now.
  • Myth: acne comes from dirty skin, and toothpaste or lemon juice fixes spots. Both irritate and can darken marks.
  • Hype: 'detox' teas, eczema 'cures' and supplements sold online. If it promises to cure eczema or blood pressure, be sceptical.
  • Wrist and finger BP gadgets and smartwatches are often inaccurate. Use a validated upper-arm cuff.
